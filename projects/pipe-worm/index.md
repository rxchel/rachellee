# Bio-Inspired Pipe Inspection Robot for Aging Water Infrastructure

**Engineering Design · Prototyping · Mechanical Design · CAD**

![Animation of the transportation mechanism of the pipe inspection robot.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/pipe-worm/image16.gif)

*Animation of the transportation mechanism of the pipe inspection robot.*

## Project Overview

This project developed a **bio-inspired pipe inspection and relining robot** designed
to address the challenges associated with aging water infrastructure.

Aging water pipelines experience frequent failures and require substantial 
repair and maintenance costs. Deteriorated pipes can also compromise access 
to essential resources such as clean water and create public health concerns. 
Our team designed a robotic system intended to travel through existing pipelines, 
navigate bends, and assist with pipe inspection and relining without requiring 
extensive excavation.

The project focused on developing a robot that could navigate standard main-line 
water pipes while remaining flexible, lightweight, and energy efficient. 
Our design requirements included navigating 6–16 inch diameter pipes, traveling 
at least 20 feet per hour, bending at least 90°, weighing less than 20 pounds, 
and operating for at least two hours. We also prioritized waterproofing and 
minimizing the use of electronics.

## My Contributions

I contributed to the team's **problem definition, design development, 
prototyping, and evaluation** throughout the engineering design process.

### Problem Definition & Design Requirements

I helped frame the engineering problem around the challenges associated with 
deteriorating water infrastructure and translate those challenges into 
measurable design requirements.

The team established quantitative criteria for pipe compatibility, speed, 
flexibility, weight, battery life, waterproofing, and electronic complexity. 
These requirements provided a framework for evaluating potential designs and 
determining whether our final concept could address the intended application.

I also contributed to communicating the motivation behind the project, 
emphasizing the need for technologies that can improve the inspection and 
maintenance of aging water infrastructure.

### Biomimetic Design

![Ribbon worm proboscis]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/pipe-worm/image15.gif)

*The ribbon worm proboscis in action.*

I contributed to exploring biological systems as inspiration for the robot's 
design.

Our primary inspiration was the **ribbon worm (Nemertea)**, whose flexible proboscis 
and crawling motion suggested a way to combine pipe relining with flexible 
locomotion. We adapted this concept into a robot with an inflatable, sectional 
structure capable of moving through confined spaces.

This approach allowed us to consider alternatives to conventional wheeled or 
tracked robots, which can be difficult to adapt to narrow, curved pipes.

### Prototype Development

I contributed to the development and evaluation of multiple locomotion concepts, 
including inchworm-style movement, tracks, wheels, and inflatable sections.

The team compared these approaches based on criteria including pipe compatibility, 
speed, flexibility, portability, material requirements, power consumption, 
feasibility, and ease of use. The inflatable-section approach was ultimately 
selected because it provided the flexibility and air-powered actuation needed to 
navigate the intended environment.

We developed an initial working prototype and iterated on the design to improve 
its locomotion and ability to move through a pipe.

### Experimental Testing & Analysis

I contributed to testing the final prototype and analyzing its performance 
against the established design requirements.

The final prototype used an air pump to inflate and deflate individual sections, 
producing an inchworm-like locomotion mechanism. Testing showed an average 
pressure of approximately 3 PSI, with approximately 8 seconds required for 
inflation and 8 seconds for deflation. The prototype traveled approximately 154 
seconds per meter, corresponding to about **24 meters per hour** (79 feet per hour).

These results indicated that the prototype exceeded the minimum speed 
requirement of 20 feet per hour.

Testing also identified an important limitation: the balloon material 
experienced approximately **6.66% deformation after 20 uses**, indicating that 
material durability would need to be addressed before the design could be used 
in a practical application.

## Design Process

### Identifying the Problem

The project began with an investigation of aging water infrastructure and the 
challenges associated with inspecting and maintaining pipelines.

Rather than designing a system around a predetermined technology, we established 
measurable requirements based on the physical constraints of the intended 
environment.

### Exploring Design Alternatives

We evaluated several possible locomotion mechanisms, including:

- Inchworm-style locomotion
- Tracks
- Wheels
- Inflatable sectional movement

Conventional tracked and wheeled systems offered advantages in speed and 
intuitive operation but presented challenges related to flexibility, energy 
consumption, and fitting inside pipes. The inchworm-inspired approach offered 
greater flexibility and lower energy requirements.

### Developing the Pipe Worm

The final concept combined two biological inspirations from ribbon worms:

- A proboscis-inspired pipe liner
- Crawling-inspired locomotion

The resulting design used inflatable sections to create sequential movement 
through the pipe while maintaining a flexible form factor.

## Results

The final prototype successfully demonstrated air-powered locomotion through a
pipe using sequentially inflatable sections.

Testing produced:

| Performance Metric | Result |
|---|---:|
| Average inflation pressure | ~3 PSI |
| Inflation time | ~8 seconds |
| Deflation time | ~8 seconds |
| Travel speed | ~24 m/hr |
| Travel speed | ~79 ft/hr |
| Material deformation after 20 uses | ~6.66% |

The prototype's measured speed was approximately **four times the minimum design requirement**, 
demonstrating the potential of inflatable locomotion for pipe inspection applications.

However, repeated testing revealed deformation of the balloon material. 
Improving material durability and consistency would therefore be an important 
next step in developing the prototype into a more robust engineering system.

## Project Takeaways

This project gave me experience with the end-to-end workflow of an engineering 
design project:

problem identification → design requirements → biomimetic ideation → prototype 
development → testing → quantitative evaluation → design iteration → technical 
communication

Through this work, I developed practical experience in engineering design, 
biomimicry, prototyping, experimental testing, quantitative analysis, and 
team-based problem solving.

Most importantly, the project taught me how to translate a real-world 
infrastructure problem into measurable engineering requirements and use
